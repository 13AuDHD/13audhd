Living between destinations.

What home looks like when it has four wheels, a worn map, and a changing view.

Home is not always a place you return to. Sometimes it is the thing carrying you forward.

For me, home has four wheels, a high-mileage engine, a mattress cut to fit, a kitchen built behind the driver’s seat, and a map that has been folded and unfolded enough times to lose its original shape. Home is Horizon, my 2004 Chevrolet Suburban, and the changing patch of ground beneath it.

Some mornings I wake beneath pine trees. Other days begin in a parking lot, beside a storage unit, at a repair shop, or on the shoulder of a road while I listen for a sound that was not there yesterday. The view changes constantly. The responsibilities do not.

Photographs make this life look simple. A truck beneath a wide mountain sky. Coffee beside a quiet campsite. Snow on the trees. A dirt road disappearing toward somewhere empty. Those moments are real, and they are part of why I chose this life.

They are not the whole story.

The photograph ends at the edge of the frame

A photograph can show a beautiful campsite without showing the hour spent leveling the truck, moving gear, checking the weather, finding a signal, or deciding whether the road will still be passable after rain.

It can show dinner cooking beside the mountains without showing the power calculation behind it. How much battery remains? How much sun is expected tomorrow? Will the refrigerator run through the night? Is there enough water to wash the pan, or does that water need to be saved for drinking?

It can show Horizon on a rough forest road without showing the worn suspension parts, the air pressure in the rear shocks, the fuel gauge moving faster than expected, or the quiet mental list of everything that could break far from help.

The glamorous photograph usually begins after the problem has already been solved.

That does not make the photograph dishonest. Beauty and difficulty can exist in the same place. The problem comes when only the beauty is visible and the work disappears.

Every problem becomes your problem

In a house or apartment, many systems are quietly maintained by someone else. Water arrives through a pipe. Electricity comes through the wall. Trash leaves on a schedule. Heat, refrigeration, plumbing, security, storage, internet, and weather protection are built into the structure.

In a vehicle, those systems become smaller, more visible, and much more personal.

Water is not an invisible utility. It is a ten-gallon tank that has to be filled, carried, monitored, and rationed. Electricity is a battery percentage, a solar forecast, a folding panel, and the number of watts being pulled by whatever is running at that moment. Refrigeration is an appliance that must keep working even when the road shakes every connector and component inside it.

When something fails, there is no maintenance office to call. The problem lives in the same small space that I do.

Road rule: A small problem handled early is usually cheaper, safer, and less disruptive than the same problem ignored until it becomes urgent.

That means learning how things work. It means carrying tools, spare fittings, hose clamps, tire plugs, electrical supplies, fluids, recovery gear, and enough knowledge to make a temporary repair when a permanent one is not immediately possible.

It also means knowing when not to improvise. Some problems can be patched beside a road. Others require stopping, spending money, changing plans, and admitting that continuing would be reckless.

Freedom still has a budget

The road can feel open, but it is never free.

Fuel decides how far I can move. Repairs decide whether I can move at all. Food, water, insurance, phone service, storage, maintenance, and the cost of replacing equipment all exist whether the view is beautiful or not.

Sometimes the hard choice is between traveling somewhere new and keeping enough money available for the repair I know is coming. Sometimes it is deciding whether a campsite is worth the extra fuel. Sometimes it is choosing a cheaper meal because the truck needs another part.

A paid-off vehicle can provide a kind of freedom that a financed apartment or car never did, but that freedom is not the absence of expense. It is the responsibility to decide which expense matters most.

Living with less does not remove hard choices. It places those choices closer together.

There are days when the choice is obvious. Fix the truck. Buy the fuel. Refill the water. Stay somewhere safe.

Other choices are harder because both options matter. Replace a worn part now or save for a larger repair. Stay near town for work or travel somewhere quieter for my health. Carry something useful or protect the limited living space I have left.

Every object, mile, and dollar has weight.

Space requires negotiation

Living inside a Suburban means every piece of space has more than one job.

The bed sits above storage. The kitchen occupies the area behind the driver’s seat. The refrigerator replaced the center console. Roof boxes hold recovery gear, tools, camp supplies, extra food, coffee, a tarp, and the things that would otherwise crowd the living area.

Adding something often means removing something else.

A larger water tank provides more time away from town, but it adds weight and consumes space. More tools increase self-sufficiency, but they also increase the load carried over every mile. Communications gear adds safety and connection, but it requires antennas, wiring, power, mounting space, and attention.

Comfort is also negotiated. I cannot carry every version of every item. I cannot keep every possession nearby. I have to decide what supports daily life, what handles emergencies, and what is only occupying space because I am afraid to let it go.

That process is not always liberating. Sometimes it is frustrating. Sometimes the object I remove is something I genuinely wanted to keep. Small-space living is often described as freedom from possessions, but in practice it can feel like a long sequence of compromises.

Weather is part of the house

In a conventional home, weather usually stays outside. In Horizon, weather becomes part of the interior experience.

Cold settles into the glass and metal. Heat builds under the roof. Wind moves the truck. Rain changes the road beneath it. Snow can turn an ordinary exit into a decision that needs to be made before the storm arrives.

A beautiful remote campsite can become dangerous if the temperature drops farther than expected, a road washes out, or mud makes the route back impossible. A shaded summer camp may be comfortable but poor for solar charging. A sunny site may provide excellent power while turning the interior into an oven.

The best location is rarely perfect. It is the place where the tradeoffs are manageable.

This lifestyle teaches attention. Clouds matter. Wind direction matters. The condition of the road matters. The angle of the sun, the amount of fuel, the nearest town, and the distance to pavement all become part of choosing where to sleep.

Plans are written in pencil

The road does not care how carefully I planned the day.

A campsite may be occupied. A gate may be closed. A road may be rougher than the map suggested. A mechanical problem may require turning around. Work may be slower than expected. Weather may make the next destination irrelevant.

I have learned to plan with alternatives.

There is the place I hope to go, the place I can use if the first one fails, and the place I can safely stop if the entire day falls apart.

Flexibility sounds adventurous until it is required. There are moments when changing plans feels like failure, especially after spending time, fuel, and energy trying to reach somewhere specific. But stubbornness is expensive on the road.

Turning around can be the right decision. Staying near town can be the right decision. Waiting for weather, repairing something before continuing, or abandoning a route can all be signs of good judgment rather than defeat.

Loneliness and solitude are not the same

I seek out quiet places because quiet helps me function. The woods, mountains, and open roads give me distance from noise, pressure, crowds, and the constant feeling of being watched.

Solitude can be restorative. It can also become loneliness without warning.

There are days when being far from everyone feels exactly right. There are other days when the same distance feels heavier. A weak signal, an empty road, and a long night can feel peaceful or isolating depending on what I carried into the evening.

Staying connected requires effort. Starlink, cellular equipment, radios, messages, and occasional trips back into town are not just conveniences. They are part of maintaining a life that still includes other people.

Independence is useful. Total isolation is not the goal.

The difficult parts do not cancel the good ones

None of this means I regret choosing this life.

The difficult parts are not evidence that the beautiful parts are fake. They are the cost, work, and uncertainty surrounding them.

I still wake beneath trees. I still open the door to places that would never fit outside an apartment window. I still have mornings when the first thing I see is fog moving through a valley or sunlight touching a mountain ridge.

Horizon gives me a home that can move toward quiet, weather, opportunity, work, or safety. It carries my bed, kitchen, tools, power, water, food, communications equipment, unfinished ideas, and the parts of my life that matter most.

It has also taught me that freedom is not the same as ease.

Home on the road is not a permanent vacation. It is a life built around movement, responsibility, and the willingness to solve the next problem.

Some days the problem is mechanical. Some days it is financial. Sometimes it is weather, space, energy, loneliness, or the difficult choice between two imperfect options.

Then there are days when everything works. The truck is level. The refrigerator is cold. The batteries are charged. The road is dry. Dinner is easy. The signal is strong enough. The night is quiet.

Those days feel simple because so many systems are quietly holding together at once.

Home is the system I keep rebuilding

Home used to mean an address. Now it means a collection of systems, routines, and familiar objects arranged inside a machine I trust because I have spent so much time learning its weaknesses.

It is the mattress shaped around the interior. The coffee stored where I can reach it. The sound of the refrigerator cycling at night. The battery display glowing in the dark. The roof boxes above me. The tools I hope not to need. The map showing where I have been and the roads I am still thinking about.

It is knowing where everything belongs after dark. It is recognizing the sound of the engine, the feel of the suspension, and the small changes that may mean something needs attention.

Home is not any single destination.

It is the thing that remains familiar while the view outside keeps changing.

The road can be difficult, expensive, uncomfortable, and unpredictable. It can demand sacrifices that never appear in the photographs. It can force decisions I would rather postpone and expose every weak point in both the vehicle and the plan.

It can also lead to a quiet road beneath the mountains, with enough water, enough power, and nowhere else I need to be that night.

I live somewhere between those two truths.
